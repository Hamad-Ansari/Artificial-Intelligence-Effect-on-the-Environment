Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has become a transformative force in today’s world, driving innovations in healthcare, finance, transportation, and beyond. From virtual assistants in our pockets to complex algorithms optimizing global supply chains, AI is shaping how we live and work. However, as AI systems grow more powerful and pervasive, concerns are rising about their environmental impact. The very computing infrastructure that enables AI—vast data centers and specialized hardware—consumes enormous amounts of energy and resources. In fact, the energy used by data centers worldwide is projected to skyrocket in the coming years due to AI demand . At the same time, AI is also being harnessed as a tool to help solve environmental challenges, from climate modeling to sustainable agriculture. This blog will explore both sides of this coin: the positive effects of AI in tackling environmental issues and the negative effects of AI’s own resource consumption and emissions. Real-world examples, data-driven insights, and case studies will illustrate how AI can be both a boon and a burden for the planet. The goal is to provide a balanced perspective and highlight how responsible AI development and green technologies can mitigate AI’s footprint while maximizing its environmental benefits.

Positive Effects of AI on the Environment

AI is increasingly being deployed as a powerful ally in environmental sustainability efforts. Its ability to analyze vast datasets, recognize patterns, and optimize complex systems makes it invaluable for addressing some of the biggest environmental challenges of our time. Below, we examine several key areas where AI is having a positive impact, along with real-world examples and outcomes.

Climate Change Modeling and Forecasting

One of the most critical applications of AI is in climate change modeling and forecasting. Traditional climate models are computationally intensive and can take weeks to simulate decades of climate scenarios. AI techniques, especially machine learning, are helping scientists accelerate and improve these models. For example, researchers have developed AI models that can simulate 100 years of climate patterns in just 25 hours – about 25 times faster than conventional methods . This generative AI model, known as Spherical Diffusion, runs on standard GPU clusters and can produce century-scale climate projections far more efficiently than supercomputer-driven models . The implications are profound: faster and more accessible climate simulations can enable scientists and policymakers to run many more scenarios and make better-informed decisions to combat climate change. In another breakthrough, a team at the University of Washington created an AI model that can simulate 1,000 years of climate in roughly 12 hours, a task that would take a state-of-the-art supercomputer 90 days to complete . Such AI-driven climate models not only save time and energy in computation, but they also help in understanding long-term climate variability and extreme events. By improving the accuracy and speed of climate projections, AI is enhancing our ability to predict and prepare for the impacts of global warming.

AI is also being used to forecast extreme weather events with greater precision. In 2023, Google DeepMind introduced a model called GraphCast that can predict global weather up to 10 days ahead more accurately than the current leading forecasting systems . This AI model, trained on historical weather data, can generate forecasts in a fraction of the time of traditional physics-based models, potentially improving early warnings for hurricanes, floods, and heatwaves. These advances in AI-driven climate and weather prediction are crucial for developing effective climate change mitigation and adaptation strategies. By providing faster, more accurate forecasts, AI helps communities prepare for disasters and allows scientists to explore climate solutions more efficiently. As climate researcher Dale Durran noted regarding the new 1000-year AI climate simulation, the model not only dramatically reduces computational cost but also makes complex climate experiments accessible to many more researchers, since it can run on a single processor rather than a massive supercomputer . This democratization of climate modeling could accelerate collaborative efforts to address climate change.

Renewable Energy Optimization

Transitioning to renewable energy sources like solar and wind is essential for combating climate change, but these sources are intermittent and dependent on weather. AI is playing a pivotal role in optimizing renewable energy production and integration into the power grid. One key application is predictive forecasting: AI algorithms analyze weather patterns, satellite imagery, and historical data to predict solar irradiance and wind speeds with high accuracy. This allows energy operators to anticipate fluctuations in renewable output and balance supply and demand more effectively. For example, AI-driven forecasting can reduce solar power prediction errors by up to 30%, significantly improving grid stability and the reliability of solar energy . Similarly, in wind energy, machine learning models have been shown to improve wind power forecasts by roughly 25%, helping wind farms and grid operators make better decisions about when to store energy or adjust output . By smoothing out the variability of renewables, AI enables a higher proportion of clean energy to be used without sacrificing grid reliability.

AI is also used for optimizing the operation of renewable energy infrastructure. Companies like Siemens Gamesa employ AI and IoT sensors on wind turbines to detect early signs of mechanical stress or failure, enabling predictive maintenance that reduces downtime and waste . In solar energy, AI algorithms can adjust the angle of solar panels in real-time for maximum sunlight capture or cluster and analyze sensor data to pinpoint underperforming panels for cleaning or repair. These optimizations boost the efficiency of renewable installations, effectively squeezing more clean energy out of the same hardware. Another example comes from smart grids, where AI helps manage the flow of electricity from diverse sources. AI systems analyze real-time data on energy usage, weather conditions, and grid status to automatically adjust power distribution, reducing transmission losses and preventing overloads. In one pilot project, an AI-powered grid management system developed by Siemens and NextGen Grid was able to reduce energy waste by 20% and improve overall grid efficiency by 15% . Such improvements mean less energy is wasted and less fossil fuel backup is needed, lowering emissions.

Moreover, AI contributes to energy storage optimization – a critical component of renewable energy systems. Machine learning models predict when surplus solar or wind energy will be available and when demand will peak, guiding when to charge or discharge batteries. This maximizes the use of stored renewable energy and minimizes reliance on fossil-fueled peaker plants. In summary, AI’s ability to forecast, optimize, and learn from data is making renewable energy more reliable, efficient, and cost-effective. These improvements are accelerating the shift to a low-carbon energy future by addressing some of the key technical challenges of renewable power at scale.

Smart Agriculture for Sustainability

Agriculture is both a major contributor to environmental pressures (through greenhouse gas emissions, water use, and land degradation) and is highly vulnerable to climate change. AI-powered smart agriculture offers solutions to make farming more sustainable by reducing waste and improving resource efficiency. One of the most impactful applications is in precision agriculture, where AI helps farmers use water, fertilizers, and pesticides more judiciously. For instance, AI-driven systems analyze data from soil sensors, weather forecasts, and satellite images to determine the optimal times and amounts for irrigation. In California, a large-scale farm implemented an AI-based precision irrigation system that cut water consumption by 30% while maintaining crop yields . By delivering water exactly when and where crops need it (and no more), such systems prevent water waste and alleviate stress on local water supplies. Similarly, AI can identify pest infestations or diseases in crops at an early stage by analyzing drone or camera images, allowing farmers to treat only affected areas rather than blanket-spraying entire fields. This targeted approach reduces chemical usage and its environmental side-effects.

AI is also improving crop yield and soil health through better decision-making. Machine learning models can predict crop yields based on planting patterns, weather, and soil conditions, helping farmers plan more effectively. They can also recommend optimal crop rotations or planting schedules to improve soil fertility over time. In one case study, researchers used AI to analyze data from thousands of fields and determined that a specific crop rotation could increase yields by 10% while reducing fertilizer needs. Some AI systems even integrate with agricultural machinery: for example, tractors equipped with AI vision systems can precisely apply fertilizer only where needed, or autonomous drones can spot-spray weeds, dramatically cutting down on chemical use. According to a recent review, the adoption of AI and digital technologies in farming can lead to significant reductions in environmental impacts, such as lowering chemical runoff and greenhouse gas emissions from agriculture . These tools enable a more sustainable intensification of agriculture – producing more food with fewer inputs and less environmental harm.

Beyond field-level optimizations, AI contributes to supply chain efficiency in agriculture. Machine learning algorithms forecast market demands and optimize logistics, which helps reduce food waste. By predicting harvest yields and coordinating distribution, AI can ensure that produce gets to market or storage facilities more efficiently, minimizing spoilage. Some AI applications also focus on post-harvest processing, using computer vision to sort and grade produce, thus reducing losses. In summary, AI in agriculture is fostering a shift toward climate-smart farming: crops are grown with precision, resources are conserved, and environmental impacts are mitigated, all while maintaining or even increasing productivity. This not only helps in feeding a growing population sustainably but also reduces agriculture’s carbon footprint and strain on ecosystems.

Pollution Monitoring and Environmental Preservation

AI is proving to be a game-changer in monitoring and mitigating pollution, from tracking air quality to cleaning up our oceans. Traditional environmental monitoring often relies on sparse sensor networks and manual analysis, which can miss critical details. AI enhances this by processing vast streams of data from sensors, satellites, and even citizen science to detect patterns and anomalies in real time. For example, in the realm of air pollution monitoring, AI systems ingest data from thousands of air quality sensors and combine it with weather data and traffic patterns to accurately map pollution levels across cities. These models can identify pollution hotspots and even forecast air quality days in advance, giving authorities and the public early warning of hazardous conditions . In one study, an AI-based air quality forecasting model was able to predict PM2.5 concentrations with high accuracy, significantly outperforming previous methods . Such forecasts allow cities to take proactive measures, like issuing health advisories or adjusting traffic flow, to reduce harmful exposure. AI is also used to attribute pollution sources – by analyzing wind patterns and chemical signatures, machine learning can determine whether spikes in pollution come from industrial emissions, vehicle exhaust, or natural sources, helping regulators target the right culprits.

Water pollution is another area where AI is making a difference. Researchers have developed AI-enabled sensor systems that can detect contaminants in water in real time. One innovative solution uses a spectroscopic sensor coupled with AI to continuously analyze water samples for pollutants . This kind of system can alert officials to dangerous levels of chemicals or pathogens much faster than lab tests, enabling quicker responses to pollution incidents. AI is also employed in wastewater treatment, optimizing the treatment process to remove pollutants more efficiently and reducing energy use of treatment plants. In addition, AI algorithms help in monitoring large water bodies: for instance, satellite image analysis with AI can spot oil spills or algal blooms over vast ocean areas that would be hard to detect by eye. The United Nations has noted that AI is being used to map destructive practices like illegal sand dredging in rivers and coastal areas and to chart emissions of methane from oil and gas facilities . Methane, a potent greenhouse gas, can be detected by AI analyzing data from satellites or aircraft, helping to identify and fix leaks in fossil fuel infrastructure more promptly.

AI is also aiding conservation efforts by monitoring and protecting ecosystems. In wildlife conservation, AI-powered camera traps and drones use image recognition to identify species and even count individuals, which helps track biodiversity changes over time. Machine learning models can sift through thousands of hours of audio recordings to detect the calls of endangered species or the sounds of poachers’ activities in real time, enabling rangers to intervene. Another cutting-edge application is using AI to fight plastic pollution: computer vision systems are being deployed in recycling facilities to automatically sort plastics by type with high accuracy, improving recycling rates. AI-driven robots can also identify and pick up trash in waterways and on beaches. For example, startup companies have developed AI-equipped drones and boats that patrol water bodies and spot floating plastic debris for removal. These technologies significantly enhance the efficiency of cleanup operations. In one case, an AI vision system was able to sort recyclable materials with over 90% accuracy, far surpassing manual sorting and thereby increasing the amount of material that can be recycled instead of ending up in landfills. By automating and improving pollution monitoring and cleanup, AI is helping societies respond more effectively to environmental threats. This leads to better air and water quality, healthier ecosystems, and a greater ability to enforce environmental regulations. In short, AI is becoming an indispensable tool in the quest to preserve and restore the environment, augmenting human efforts with data-driven insights and automation.

Negative Effects of AI on the Environment

While AI offers promising solutions for environmental sustainability, it also carries a significant environmental footprint of its own. The rapid growth of AI technologies—particularly large-scale machine learning models and the infrastructure that supports them—has raised concerns about energy consumption, carbon emissions, and electronic waste. In this section, we delve into the negative environmental impacts associated with AI, including the energy demands of data centers, the carbon emissions from training and running AI systems, and the generation of electronic waste (e-waste). Understanding these challenges is crucial for developing strategies to mitigate AI’s ecological footprint.

Energy-Hungry Data Centers and Increasing Carbon Footprint

AI systems, especially those based on deep learning, require immense computational resources. This has led to a surge in demand for data centers – large facilities packed with servers and specialized AI chips – which in turn consume huge amounts of electricity. Globally, data centers currently account for about 1% to 2% of total electricity use, roughly on par with the aviation industry . However, this share is poised to grow dramatically as AI workloads expand. The International Energy Agency (IEA) estimates that electricity demand from data centers worldwide could more than double by 2030, reaching around 945 terawatt-hours (TWh) . Other analyses are even more stark: one study suggests data centers could consume up to 21% of global electricity by 2030 if AI adoption continues on its current trajectory . Much of this growth is being driven by AI: recent research indicates that AI workloads now represent as much as 20% of global data center power demand, and this fraction could approach 50% by the end of 2025 . In other words, by the middle of this decade, half of the power used by the world’s data centers could be for AI applications. This is a remarkable shift, fueled by the popularity of AI services like large language models (e.g. ChatGPT) and generative AI tools, which require constant computation on powerful servers.

The environmental concern is that this growing energy consumption translates into a growing carbon footprint, unless the electricity is generated from renewable sources. Most data centers still draw power from the grid, which in many regions is supplied by fossil fuels. As a result, the emissions from data centers are climbing. The U.S. Department of Energy reported that data centers in the United States emitted about 90 million metric tons of CO₂ in 2022, which is roughly 1.4% of the nation’s total emissions . Globally, data center CO₂ emissions are estimated to be in the hundreds of millions of tons annually, and are expected to rise alongside energy use . The training of large AI models is especially carbon-intensive. In 2019, researchers found that training a single state-of-the-art AI model could emit over 626,000 pounds (about 284 tons) of CO₂, nearly five times the lifetime emissions of an average car . Newer and larger models have only increased these numbers. Estimates suggest that training the GPT-3 language model (with 175 billion parameters) emitted on the order of 550 tons of CO₂ , and training the even larger GPT-4 may have generated on the order of 1,200 to 15,000 tons of CO₂ depending on the estimate . To put this in perspective, 15,000 tons of CO₂ is roughly equivalent to the annual emissions of 900 average U.S. households . These figures, while eye-opening, are not meant to vilify AI, but rather to underscore the scale of the energy challenge. Each query or interaction with a large AI model also has a smaller but cumulative carbon cost. For example, one analysis estimated that a single query to a generative AI model like ChatGPT might emit around 0.5 grams of CO₂ . Multiply that by millions of daily users, and the emissions add up. Tech companies are increasingly aware of these impacts. Google, for instance, noted in its 2024 sustainability report that as it integrates more AI into products, reducing emissions is challenging due to the greater intensity of AI compute . In fact, Google’s overall greenhouse gas emissions rose ~48% since 2019, a trend it partly attributes to the energy demands of AI . The key takeaway is that AI’s carbon footprint is significant and growing. This impact is an unintended consequence of the AI boom, and it highlights a critical need for greener AI practices. It’s not that AI itself is “bad” for the environment – rather, the way we power and build AI systems can be unsustainable if left unchecked. The good news is that this problem can be addressed by using renewable energy for data centers, improving hardware and algorithm efficiency, and adopting policies to incentivize sustainable computing (topics we will explore in the conclusion). By doing so, we can ensure that the AI revolution does not undermine the very climate goals that AI is helping us pursue.

The Rise of AI-Related Electronic Waste

Another often overlooked environmental impact of AI is the generation of electronic waste (e-waste). AI development and deployment rely on specialized hardware – notably graphics processing units (GPUs), tensor processing units (TPUs), and other high-performance chips – which have a finite lifespan and become obsolete as technology advances. The rapid pace of innovation in AI means that hardware can be replaced frequently, potentially leading to a surge in discarded electronics. A recent study in the journal Nature raised alarms that generative AI could contribute between 1.2 million and 5 million tons of e-waste by 2030 . To put this in perspective, 5 million tons is roughly equivalent to discarding 13 billion iPhones worth of electronics . While this scenario is a projection based on certain growth assumptions, it highlights a serious potential issue. The same study found that if AI growth follows an optimistic scenario, the cumulative e-waste from AI hardware could reach about 1.2 million tons by 2030, but under a more aggressive growth scenario it could approach 5.0 million tons . This waste includes not only the high-end chips used in data centers, but also servers, cooling equipment, and backup power supplies (like batteries) that are part of the AI infrastructure .

The e-waste problem is concerning for several reasons. Electronic waste often contains hazardous materials (lead, mercury, cadmium, etc.) that can leach into soil and water if not properly disposed of. It also represents a loss of valuable resources – many AI chips are made with precious metals and rare earth elements that require significant energy and environmental cost to mine and refine. When these devices are discarded prematurely, those resources are wasted and can end up in landfills or informal recycling operations with poor environmental controls. The rapid turnover of AI hardware can exacerbate the global e-waste crisis, which is already growing at about 3–4% per year. The Nature study noted that AI-related e-waste could grow at an astonishing 129% to 167% compound annual growth rate through 2030 in their scenarios – more than two orders of magnitude faster than conventional e-waste growth. This implies that without intervention, AI could become a significant source of e-waste in a very short time. There are signs that this trend has already begun: reports indicate that tech companies are already swapping out older GPU servers for newer, faster models to keep up with AI demand, with some estimates suggesting 80% of current AI server hardware could be scrapped by 2027 . Such rapid replacement cycles, if widespread, would indeed generate mountains of discarded equipment.

Addressing AI’s e-waste impact will require proactive strategies. One approach is hardware longevity and repurposing – designing AI hardware that lasts longer and can be upgraded, and finding ways to repurpose older hardware for less intensive tasks rather than immediately scrapping it. Another is improved recycling – ensuring that when AI hardware does reach end-of-life, it is recycled in facilities that recover valuable materials efficiently and safely. Encouragingly, AI itself can assist in recycling efforts (a case of using AI to solve an AI-related problem). For example, AI vision systems are being used in recycling plants to better sort e-waste and recover components and materials for reuse . There are also initiatives exploring the use of more sustainable materials in electronics and designing chips with easier disassembly in mind. Policymakers may also play a role, perhaps through regulations that encourage manufacturers to take back and recycle AI equipment or to meet standards for durability. In summary, while AI-driven e-waste is a growing concern, it is not an insurmountable one. By implementing circular economy principles – reduce, reuse, recycle – the AI industry can mitigate its waste footprint. The challenge will be to keep hardware innovation aligned with environmental sustainability, so that the benefits of AI do not come at the cost of massive piles of electronic waste.

Water Consumption and Other Environmental Strains

In addition to energy and e-waste, AI infrastructure imposes demands on other natural resources, particularly water. Data centers, which are the backbone of AI services, require cooling to prevent servers from overheating. Many data centers use water-intensive cooling systems, such as chillers and evaporative cooling towers, to maintain optimal temperatures. As AI workloads increase the heat output in data centers, water consumption for cooling tends to rise as well. A medium-sized data center can consume on the order of 110 million gallons of water per year for cooling – roughly equivalent to the annual water use of 800 households . Larger hyperscale data centers (the kind operated by major tech companies) use significantly more. In the United States, for example, data center water use tripled from about 5.6 billion gallons in 2014 to an estimated 17 billion gallons in 2023, with the largest 100 or so facilities accounting for the bulk of this usage . Globally, data centers are estimated to consume around 560 billion liters of water annually, and this could rise to 1.2 trillion liters by 2030 as AI adoption grows . These figures highlight a significant water footprint, especially in regions where water is scarce. Cooling water is often drawn from local rivers, lakes, or underground aquifers, and while much of it is returned, a substantial portion can be lost to evaporation. In fact, data centers typically evaporate about 80% of the water they use for cooling, releasing it into the atmosphere as vapor . This means that the water is not available for reuse in the local environment. The remaining 20% might be discharged as wastewater after treatment, but the net effect is a depletion of freshwater resources in the area. There have been instances where planned data center projects have raised concerns among local communities and regulators due to their potential water draw. For example, in some western U.S. states facing drought, proposals for new AI data centers have been met with opposition over fears of straining already limited water supplies . The strain on local water systems is a very real environmental and social issue – one data center might use as much water in a day as a small city, which can be problematic in arid regions or during drought conditions. Aside from cooling, water is also indirectly consumed in the production of the electricity that powers data centers (for instance, thermoelectric power plants use water for cooling as well) and in the manufacturing of the computer hardware. Producing a single high-end semiconductor chip can require thousands of gallons of ultra-pure water for fabrication. Thus, the water footprint of AI extends through the supply chain.

To mitigate AI’s water impact, the industry is exploring water-saving technologies in data centers. Some companies are shifting to air-cooling or liquid immersion cooling systems that use less water or even none at all. Others are locating data centers in cooler climates or underground to reduce cooling needs. There’s also interest in using non-potable water (like treated wastewater or seawater) for cooling where possible, so as not to compete with drinking water supplies. Additionally, techniques like free cooling (using outside air during cold weather) and heat reuse (using the waste heat from data centers to warm buildings or for industrial processes) can reduce both energy and water consumption. On the policy side, some governments are starting to consider water use in data center permitting and encouraging water recycling on-site. It’s worth noting that while AI infrastructure has a water footprint, AI tools can also help manage water resources more efficiently (for example, optimizing irrigation as discussed earlier, or predicting water demand and leaks in smart cities). This creates an opportunity to use AI to offset some of its own impacts. In conclusion, water consumption is a significant environmental strain associated with AI’s growth, particularly in water-scarce regions. Reducing this impact will be crucial for sustainable AI development. By implementing innovative cooling solutions and responsible water management, the AI industry can work to ensure that its thirst for water does not contribute to water scarcity or environmental harm.

Conclusion: Striving for a Sustainable AI Future

Artificial Intelligence stands at the nexus of opportunity and responsibility when it comes to the environment. On one hand, AI is a powerful tool that can help humanity tackle pressing ecological challenges – from accelerating climate research and enabling clean energy to optimizing resource use in agriculture and monitoring pollution. On the other hand, the very deployment of AI technologies carries environmental costs in the form of high energy consumption, carbon emissions, and resource depletion. The key takeaway is that AI’s impact on the environment is not predetermined; it depends on how we develop and use these technologies. With intentional effort, we can maximize AI’s positive contributions while minimizing its negatives. This requires a commitment to responsible AI development and the adoption of green technologies across the AI lifecycle.

One of the most important steps is to ensure that AI systems are powered by sustainable energy. Tech companies and data center operators are increasingly investing in renewable energy sources like solar and wind to run their servers. For instance, Google, Microsoft, and others have pledged to use 100% renewable energy for their operations and to achieve carbon-neutral AI. These commitments, if followed through, would drastically cut the carbon footprint of AI. In fact, using renewable energy could reduce the emissions of training a large AI model by 90% or more, since the main source of carbon output is the fossil fuel electricity powering the computers. Another strategy is improving energy efficiency in AI algorithms and hardware. Researchers are developing more efficient AI models (through techniques like model compression, pruning, and using smaller but smarter architectures) that achieve the same results with fewer computations. There are already examples of AI models that have been distilled or simplified to run on less powerful devices, reducing the need for massive data centers for every application. On the hardware side, companies are designing specialized AI chips that perform computations with less energy, and exploring novel technologies like neuromorphic computing which mimic the brain’s efficient architecture. Even small efficiency gains can have a large cumulative impact given the scale of AI deployment.

Addressing AI’s environmental footprint also involves circular economy practices. This means designing AI hardware for longevity, repairability, and recyclability. Manufacturers can extend product lifecycles and offer upgrade paths so that hardware doesn’t become obsolete overnight. When it does reach end-of-life, robust recycling programs can recover valuable materials and prevent hazardous waste. The AI industry can lead by example in implementing take-back programs and supporting e-waste recycling innovations (some of which, ironically, involve using AI to improve recycling processes). Water usage, too, can be mitigated with design choices like waterless cooling and strategic location of data centers. Some firms are experimenting with underwater data centers or using the heat generated to warm greenhouses or homes, creating a more sustainable ecosystem around the technology.

Policy and governance will play a role in steering AI toward sustainability. Governments and international bodies can incentivize green AI through regulations and standards – for example, by requiring large AI deployments to report their energy and carbon footprint, or by offering tax breaks for AI projects that use renewable energy or demonstrate high energy efficiency. There is also a push for an “AI environmental impact assessment” to be conducted alongside traditional AI ethics assessments, ensuring that environmental considerations are part of the decision-making process for new AI systems . Academia and industry coalitions are developing guidelines for sustainable AI, encouraging researchers to publish the energy costs of their models and to explore eco-friendly AI solutions.

Crucially, we must continue to leverage AI itself as a solution to environmental problems. The positive applications of AI – in climate modeling, renewable energy, conservation, and more – are invaluable in the global effort to achieve sustainability. In many cases, the environmental benefits of using AI (such as enabling a faster transition to clean energy or preventing ecological disasters) can far outweigh the environmental costs of the AI systems, especially if those systems are made efficient and green. It’s a matter of balancing the scales: ensuring that AI’s carbon footprint is a small price to pay for the larger environmental gains it facilitates. In conclusion, AI’s impact on the environment is a double-edged sword, but with wisdom and foresight, we can dull the negative edge. By embracing renewable energy, innovating for efficiency, and adhering to sustainable practices, the AI community can significantly reduce the environmental burden of these technologies. At the same time, expanding the use of AI for climate action and conservation will amplify the positive effects. The vision of the future is one where AI and environmental sustainability go hand in hand – where intelligent algorithms help protect the planet and are themselves powered and built in ways that respect the planet’s limits. Achieving this balance will require ongoing commitment from technologists, businesses, policymakers, and users alike. The promise of AI is too great to ignore, and with concerted effort, we can ensure that this powerful tool becomes a force for good that helps usher in a greener and more sustainable world .
